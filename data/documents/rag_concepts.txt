Retrieval-Augmented Generation - Study Notes
============================================

Retrieval-Augmented Generation, usually shortened to RAG, is a technique for
making a large language model answer questions using a private collection of
documents instead of only what it memorised during training.

A language model on its own has two problems. First, its knowledge is frozen at
the moment training finished, so it cannot know about recent events. Second, it
knows nothing about documents that were never published on the internet, such
as internal wikis, contracts or support tickets. RAG solves both by fetching
the relevant text first and handing it to the model as context.


The five stages of a RAG pipeline
---------------------------------

1. Ingestion. Documents are read, cleaned and split into chunks.

2. Embedding. Each chunk is converted into a vector of floating point numbers
   by an embedding model. Similar meaning produces similar vectors.

3. Storage. The vectors are written into a vector database such as ChromaDB,
   together with the original chunk text and metadata.

4. Retrieval. The user question is embedded with the same model, and the
   database returns the chunks whose vectors are closest to the question
   vector. Closeness is normally measured with cosine similarity.

5. Generation. The retrieved chunks are inserted into a prompt, and the
   language model is instructed to answer using only that context. The answer
   is returned to the user together with the sources it came from.


Why chunking matters
--------------------

Chunks are usually between 200 and 1000 characters. If a chunk is too small it
loses the surrounding context and the model cannot tell what it refers to. If a
chunk is too large the embedding averages several different topics together and
retrieval becomes imprecise.

Overlapping chunks help at the seam. If a sentence that answers a question is
cut in half between two chunks, both halves are embedded without their other
half, and neither retrieves well. Repeating the last 10 to 20 percent of a
chunk at the start of the next one means the sentence always appears complete
in at least one chunk.

Metadata such as the document name, the page number, the author and the date
should always be stored alongside a chunk. Metadata is what lets you filter
retrieval to one document, show citations to the user, and delete or update a
single document without rebuilding the whole index.


How relevance is scored
-----------------------

ChromaDB stores vectors and computes a distance between them. With cosine
distance, identical directions score 0, orthogonal vectors score 1 and opposite
vectors score 2. Similarity is simply 1 minus distance, so a score of 0.82
means the chunk is quite close to the question.

Empirically, cosine similarity scores above 0.7 for a general purpose
sentence embedding model usually indicate the chunk is genuinely on topic.
Scores below 0.4 usually mean the chunk should not have been retrieved at all,
and a threshold is worth adding to filter them out before spending tokens on
generation.


Choosing top-k
--------------

top-k is the number of chunks sent to the model. Too few and the answer is
grounded in incomplete information. Too many and the model is distracted by
irrelevant text, the prompt grows more expensive and the answer gets vaguer.
Three to six chunks is the usual sweet spot for a first version of a system.


Retrieval quality is the real bottleneck
----------------------------------------

In practice most bad answers are caused by retrieval, not by the language
model. The most common causes are chunks that were split badly, questions that
use different vocabulary from the documents, and an embedding model that is too
weak for the domain. Before replacing the language model, print the retrieved
chunks and read them. If the answer is not in the retrieved text, no prompt
engineering can fix it.


Useful next steps
-----------------

Hybrid search combines vector similarity with keyword search such as BM25 and
usually improves recall on rare terms and product names. Reranking passes the
top 50 retrieved chunks through a cross-encoder that scores each pair of
question and chunk together, which is slower but far more accurate than a
bi-encoder. Evaluation means building a small set of question and expected
answer pairs, running the pipeline over them, and measuring whether the correct
chunk was retrieved, so that changes to chunking or to the model can be judged
with numbers instead of vibes.
